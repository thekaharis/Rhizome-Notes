\section{Spatial structure in neural operators and the U-Net}
\label{sec:spatial-structure}
% =====================================================================

\Cref{sec:fno-limitations} showed that the FNO represents a field through global, stationary, band-limited modes plus a pointwise map. By \emph{spatial structure} we mean everything this leaves out: locality (interactions that are strong between nearby points and weak between distant ones), an explicit hierarchy of scales, and interactions whose strength depends on where in the field they happen. Reionization fields need all three. Ionized bubbles span sizes from a few cells to a sizeable fraction of the box, their boundaries are sharp on the grid scale, and whether two regions interact depends on the ionization state between them. This section reviews how existing architectures build in such structure, with the U-Net as the main example, and identifies what none of them provide.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Inductive biases: locality, scale and stationarity}
\label{sec:inductive-biases}

Each layer of a neural operator can be written in the kernel form of \cref{eq:kernel-integral}. Architectures differ mainly in which kernels $\kappa(r,r',\theta)$ they allow, and these restrictions are their inductive biases. Three properties are useful for comparing them.

\paragraph{Receptive field.} A convolutional neural network (CNN) uses kernels with compact support of a few grid cells, $\kappa(r-r')=0$ for $\abs{r-r'}$ larger than the stencil. A single layer is therefore strictly local; the receptive field grows only linearly with depth. The FNO is the opposite extreme: every Fourier layer couples every point to every other point.

\paragraph{Scale.} A plain CNN or FNO processes the field at one resolution. Neither has a built-in notion of coarse and fine scales; scale-dependent behaviour has to be learned implicitly. Multi-scale architectures instead process the field at several resolutions and combine the results, so that large-scale context and small-scale detail are handled by separate parts of the network.

\paragraph{Stationarity and state dependence.} CNNs, FNOs and U-Nets all use kernels that depend only on $r-r'$: the same filter is applied everywhere, and the coupling between two points is fixed once training ends. Attention mechanisms \citep{vaswani2017attention} and graph kernel networks \citep{li2020graph} relax this by letting the kernel depend on the features at $r$ and $r'$, so that the coupling becomes \emph{state-dependent}. The price is cost: a dense state-dependent kernel requires $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ operations, which is prohibitive for three-dimensional grids with $N\sim10^{7}$ cells.

\Cref{tab:inductive-biases} summarizes these properties for the architectures discussed in this report.

\subsection{The U-Net}
\label{sec:unet}

The U-Net \citep{ronneberger2015unet} was introduced for biomedical image segmentation, a task that, like predicting an ionization field, requires sharp, pixel-accurate boundaries informed by large-scale context. It consists of three parts (\cref{fig:unet}).

\paragraph{Contracting path.} The encoder applies a stack of small convolutions followed by a nonlinearity, then halves the resolution by pooling, and repeats. Denoting the feature map at level $s$ by $e_s$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:unet-encoder}
  e_0 = C_0(a), \qquad e_{s} = C_s\big(\mathrm{Down}(e_{s-1})\big), \quad s = 1,\dots,S,
\end{equation}
where each $C_s$ is a block of $3\times3$ convolutions with nonlinearities. The number of channels is typically doubled at each level, so the network trades spatial resolution for feature richness. Because every level operates on a grid that is coarser by a factor of two, a $3\times3$ convolution at level $s$ spans $3\cdot2^{s}$ cells of the original grid. The receptive field therefore grows exponentially with the number of levels instead of linearly with depth.

\paragraph{Bottleneck.} At the coarsest level, $e_S$, the field is represented on a grid of $N/2^{S}$ points per dimension with many channels. Here a few convolutions see a large fraction of the domain and capture large-scale context.

\paragraph{Expanding path and skip connections.} The decoder upsamples step by step and, at each level, concatenates the upsampled features with the encoder features of the same resolution:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:unet-decoder}
  d_S = e_S, \qquad d_s = C'_s\big(\big[\,\mathrm{Up}(d_{s+1}),\ e_s\,\big]\big), \quad s = S-1,\dots,0,
\end{equation}
and a final $1\times1$ convolution maps $d_0$ to the output $u$. The skip connections $e_s\to d_s$ are the key ingredient. Pooling discards the exact position of features, and upsampling alone cannot restore it. The skip connections pass the full-resolution encoder features directly to the decoder, so the network can combine \emph{what} is present (from the coarse levels) with \emph{where} it is (from the fine levels).

\paragraph{Why it captures sharp structure.} The U-Net addresses each limitation identified in \cref{sec:fno-limitations}. Its finest levels operate on the full grid with local kernels, so edges can be represented directly in physical space instead of through a truncated set of global modes. Its intermediate levels give it receptive fields at every scale between a few cells and the full box, which matches the broad range of bubble sizes. Finally, skip connections let high-frequency detail bypass the bottleneck entirely. U-Nets are therefore strong baselines for field-to-field emulation whenever sharp, localized features matter.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The price is the loss of the properties that made the FNO attractive. The convolution kernels are defined in units of grid cells, not physical length, so changing the resolution changes the physical scale each filter responds to: the U-Net is not discretization invariant, and a model trained at one resolution cannot be reused at another. The number of pooling steps also fixes which grid sizes are admissible (multiples of $2^{S}$). Global context is only available at the bottleneck and only at coarse resolution, so long-range interactions are approximated rather than represented exactly. And the U-Net is still stationary: the same filters are applied everywhere, independent of the local state of the field.

\subsection{Hybrid architectures: U-FNO and related}
\label{sec:ufno}

The complementary strengths of the FNO (global, resolution-independent) and the U-Net (local, multi-scale) suggest combining them. The U-FNO of \citet{wen2022ufno} does this inside the Fourier layer. In addition to the spectral path $\F^{-1}(R_\phi\cdot\F v)$ and the pointwise path $W v$ of \cref{fig:fno-architecture}, each \emph{U-Fourier layer} adds a small U-Net acting on $v$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ufno-layer}
  v_{\ell+1}(x) = \sigma\Big( W v_\ell(x) + \F^{-1}\big(R_\phi\cdot\F v_\ell\big)(x) + \mathrm{UNet}_\ell[v_\ell](x) \Big),
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{UNet}_\ell$ denotes the U-Net path. The spectral path supplies global, low-$k$ structure, while the U-Net path restores the high-$k$, localized detail that mode truncation removes. In the original application to multiphase flow, where saturation fronts are sharp, this substantially improved accuracy over the plain FNO \citep{wen2022ufno}.

The improvement comes at a cost that matters when comparing architectures. The U-Net path adds a large number of parameters, and a U-FNO is typically much larger than an FNO of the same width and depth. In our own comparison (\cref{sec:results}), the U-FNO reference model has roughly two orders of magnitude more parameters than the matched models. Better accuracy can therefore not be attributed to the architecture alone. We treat the U-FNO as a dense reference baseline and make architectural claims only between parameter-matched models. The U-FNO also inherits the U-Net's resolution dependence through its convolutional path, and remains stationary.

Other hybrids follow the same pattern of adding a local or multi-scale branch next to a global spectral one. They differ in which branch is used and where it is inserted, but they share the structure of \cref{eq:ufno-layer}: a sum of fixed, stationary operators.

\subsection{Open problem: structure that adapts to the field}
\label{sec:adaptive-structure}

All architectures above, apart from attention, share one property: the communication pattern between points is fixed once training is done. Whether a kernel is local or global, single- or multi-scale, the coupling between $r$ and $r'$ depends only on their separation (and the level in the hierarchy), never on the field itself. Any dependence on the current state has to be built up indirectly, through pointwise nonlinearities stacked over many layers.

For reionization, this is at odds with the physics. Ionizing photons from a source travel until they are absorbed, so whether a source at $r'$ affects a point $r$ depends on the neutral fraction along the way: through an ionized region the coupling is long-range, through neutral gas it is short. The relevant interaction kernel is therefore state-dependent. Attention can represent this but scales as $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$; the stationary architectures scale well but cannot represent it directly.

An operator suited to this problem should combine four properties: (i) global reach, so that interactions across the whole box are possible; (ii) state-dependent coupling, so that the effective connections change as the field evolves; (iii) a cost close to $\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$, so that three-dimensional grids remain tractable; and (iv) a parameterization that is independent of the grid, so that the discretization invariance of the FNO is kept. The rhizome operator, introduced in \cref{sec:rhizome}, is designed around these four requirements.
